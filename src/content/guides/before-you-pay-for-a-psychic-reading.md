Most paid readings that end in disappointment were lost before the session began — wrong specialty, no budget cap, a vague question, a platform with nobody to complain to. The reading itself is the last step of a purchase, not the first. Below are the 13 checks that decide whether your money buys a genuine reading or an expensive conversation, in the order you should run them.

**The 60-second version:**

| # | Check | Why it matters, in one line |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Book through a large, long-running platform | The platform is your only layer of consumer protection |
| 2 | Read the advisor's three platform numbers | Completed readings, rating × review count, join year beat any bio |
| 3 | Match the specialty to your question | Every advisor has a lane; booking outside it wastes the session |
| 4 | Read one in-depth review of that specific reader | Star ratings compress; a real review shows how the session actually runs |
| 5 | Calculate the session total, not the per-minute rate | Rate × realistic minutes − intro offer = the only number that counts |
| 6 | Write your questions down first | Per-minute billing punishes thinking out loud |
| 7 | Set a hard budget and a timer | Endings take 2–3 minutes; cap before you connect, not after |
| 8 | Sit somewhere private and quiet | Self-consciousness edits your questions; distraction invites fishing |
| 9 | Answer briefly; do not narrate | Every detail you volunteer is one the reader never had to produce |
| 10 | Correct the reader the moment they drift | Five minutes of uncorrected drift at $4–6/min costs $20–30 |
| 11 | Watch the clock yourself | The wrap-up belongs inside your budget |
| 12 | Know the five end-the-session red flags | Fear hooks, guarantees, urgency, off-platform moves, question-harvesting |
| 13 | Have an exit plan before you need one | Ending a bad session is a mechanical skill; rehearse it in advance |

## Stage 1: Vet the Platform and the Reader (Checks 1–5)

### 1. Is the platform big and established enough to protect you?

**Book through a large, long-running platform rather than an independent operator — the platform is the only consumer protection you have.** Kasamba and Keen have both operated since 1999, more than 25 years; Purple Garden is the mobile-first major backed by the operator behind Purple Ocean. Independents on Instagram, TikTok, or personal websites may be gifted individuals, but when the session goes wrong, there is no referee.

What scale actually buys you:

- **Itemized, escrow-style billing.** The platform holds the money, meters the minutes, and shows you the charge. Nobody "rounds up."
- **A dispute channel.** All three majors run satisfaction-style credit programs for genuinely bad sessions. An independent operator offers nothing equivalent.
- **A review system with teeth.** Platforms profit when their marketplace ratings are meaningful, so bad actors accumulate visible damage instead of disappearing and reappearing under a new name.

The limitation, stated plainly: scale filters out bad *operators*, not bad *readers*. A big platform guarantees you a referee, not a good match. That is what the next three checks are for.

### 2. What do the reader's three platform numbers say?

**An advisor's profile carries three numbers that predict session quality better than anything they write about themselves: completed readings, average rating weighed against written review count, and the year they joined.** Longevity is a market filter — an advisor who burns clients does not accumulate eight years and twenty thousand sessions on a platform that displays every rating.

How we read them, after reviewing 150+ advisors:

- **Completed readings.** Volume is repetitions. A reader with five-figure session counts has heard your question type hundreds of times and stopped being surprised by it years ago.
- **Rating × review count.** A 4.7 average across 2,000 written reviews is a stronger signal than a perfect 5.0 across 40. Small samples hide everything; large samples can only be maintained by consistently not disappointing people.
- **Advisor since.** The join year tells you whether the rating survived a market cycle. A "member since 2016" badge means a decade of clients who were free to leave and did not.

One honest limit: new is not bad. Newly listed advisors are cheaper, have shorter queues, and often give longer first sessions to build their review base. Treat newness as a pricing opportunity — not as a quality signal in either direction.

### 3. Does the reader actually specialize in your question?

**Every advisor has a lane, and booking outside it is the most common self-inflicted waste of money in this market.** A love-and-relationships reader and a career-and-money reader use the same tools on different problem shapes; a medium is practicing a different discipline entirely, not a stronger version of a love reading. Asking a career specialist whether your ex is coming back buys you a polite generalist at $5 a minute.

Match on three profile fields, in order: the stated specialty list, the tools used (tarot, astrology, clairaudience, no-tools), and the questions their actual reviews describe — the reviews show what clients really bring, which is often narrower than the bio. If your situation involves contacting someone who has passed, the distinction matters enough to read up on first: [psychic, medium, and tarot reader are three different jobs](/guides/psychic-vs-medium-vs-tarot-reader/).

### 4. Has anyone reviewed this specific reader in depth?

**Star ratings compress an entire practice into one number; a written, in-depth review of the individual advisor is the closest thing to trying before buying.** A useful single-advisor review tells you things the profile cannot: how the reader behaves in the first three minutes, whether they lead or fish, what their negative reviews actually complain about, and — most valuable — who the reader is *wrong* for.

This is the gap our own review library exists to fill: we publish long-form reviews of individual advisors, 158 of them across the three major platforms at last count, browsable from our [reviews hub](/reviews/). We are deliberately recommending no one in this article. The point of this check is the habit — never book a reader nobody has examined — not the source of the examination.

### 5. What will your first session actually cost — in total?

**Per-minute rates are decoys. The only number that matters is the session total: rate × realistic minutes, minus the new-client offer.** A realistic first session runs 15–25 minutes, and mainstream platform rates cluster around $3–8 per minute (as of September 2026) — so the honest range for a first session is $45–150 at list price, before offers.

The offers change the math substantially. Illustrative, at a $4/min mid-range reader and a 20-minute session:

| Kasamba | 3 free minutes with each new advisor + 50% off first session | 3 min free, 17 min at $2/min | ≈ $34 (list $80) |
| Keen | First 5 minutes for $1 | $1, then 15 min at $4/min | ≈ $61 (list $80) |

Offers rotate, so verify the current terms at checkout. Two structural rules hold regardless: the offer is only worth using on a reader you have already vetted (checks 2–4), and the post-offer rate is the one that matters if you plan to return. ## Stage 2: Prepare Before You Connect (Checks 6–8)

### 6. Have you written your questions down?

**Write down two to four questions, ranked by importance, phrased so the answer would be usable — on paper, before you connect.** This is the single highest-ROI item on the list. Most people burn their first five paid minutes locating their own question, and a reader cannot fix a question you have not found yet.

Phrasing is the skill. Compare:

- *"What does my love life look like?"* — unfalsifiable; invites a monologue that could apply to anyone.
- *"I am deciding between rekindling with my ex and committing to someone new. What do you see around each path?"* — a decision, two named options, and an answer you can act on.

The pattern: state the situation in one sentence, name the decision, then ask what they see. If you have not yet forced your situation into that shape, do that work first — our [reading preparation guide](/guides/how-to-prepare-for-psychic-reading/) walks the full routine, and the [first reading guide](/guides/first-psychic-reading-guide/) covers what the session itself feels like.

### 7. Have you set a hard budget and a timer?

**Decide the dollar cap before the session, and set a phone timer for three minutes before it.** The reader's job is to talk; yours is to stop. Sessions without a cap do not drift to a natural ending — they drift to the next round number of your account balance.

The mechanics: pick the cap ($25 and $50 are sensible first-session tiers given the math in check 5), then subtract three minutes for the wrap-up, because endings always take two to three minutes — a summary, a closing exchange, the disconnect. When the timer fires, you are in the landing pattern, not still cruising. This pairs with check 11: the platform shows a running total, but your own timer is the one you will actually obey.

### 8. Is your environment private and quiet?

**Take the reading in a bedroom or small living room with the door closed and headphones in — never in a car, café, open-plan office, or with other people in earshot.** Two mechanisms make this matter more than it looks.

First, self-consciousness edits. You will be discussing relationships, money, or family out loud; if a roommate or stranger can hear you, you will ask a blander question than the one you are paying to ask. Second, divided attention reads as vagueness. A reader who hears traffic, keyboard noise, or half your attention gets degraded input and — the less scrupulous kind — an open invitation to fish. You want 15–25 minutes that are uninterrupted by construction, not by willpower.

## Stage 3: Run the Session (Checks 9–11)

### 9. Are you answering briefly instead of narrating?

**Answer in sentences, not paragraphs. You are paying for the reader's words — and every detail you volunteer is one they never had to produce.** Cold reading, the technique behind most convincing fake psychics, runs entirely on client feedback: offer a probable statement, watch what you react to, then build on your reaction. The less you feed the loop, the more evidential every genuine hit becomes.

This is not an instruction to be adversarial. Confirm what lands, correct what misses, and stop there. "Yes, that fits" is a complete answer. The narrative version — names, dates, backstory, your theory of what it means — is you doing the reading for them at your own expense. The full mechanics, including the three cold-reading techniques you will encounter most, are in [how to spot a fake psychic](/guides/how-to-spot-fake-psychic/).

### 10. Are you correcting the reader the moment they drift?

**Redirect at minute three, not minute eighteen.** Readers pattern-match to the most common question in their lane; if you asked about a job decision and they open on your ex, that is not insight — it is a default setting. Say so immediately: *"That is not the area I asked about — can we go back to the job situation?"*

The politeness tax is real and measurable: five minutes of uncorrected drift at $4–6 per minute is $20–30 spent on an answer to a question you did not ask. A capable reader recalibrates on correction and often sharpens noticeably — the correction gives them the feedback channel their gift actually needs. A reader who cannot be redirected has just told you what the remaining minutes are worth, and "I did not want to be rude" is the most expensive sentence in this industry.

### 11. Are you watching the clock yourself?

**Track your own minutes and put the wrap-up inside your budget.** When your timer from check 7 fires, ask for the summary directly: *"In the time we have left, what is the one thing I should take from this?"* That question forces compression while there is still time to benefit from it, and the answer is usually the most concentrated thirty seconds of the session.

What you are defending against is not malice — it is the structural fact that a per-minute reader has no economic reason to end the session, so the ending is always your job. The clients who feel readings are "a scam" are disproportionately the ones who let the meter decide the length.

## Stage 4: Red Flags and the Exit (Checks 12–13)

### 12. Do you know the five red flags that mean end the session now?

**Five behaviors end a session on their own, regardless of how well it was going before.** Each converts a reading into a sales process:

| The flag | What it sounds like | Why it ends the session |
|---|---|---|
| The fear hook | "I sense a curse / negative attachment — I can clear it, but it takes extra work" | A diagnosis whose treatment costs more money is an upsell, not a reading |
| The guarantee | "He will come back within three weeks, I promise you" | No legitimate practitioner guarantees outcomes; certainty is the tell |
| The urgency push | "You need to book a follow-up tonight, before the energy shifts" | Manufactured pressure is the product being sold |
| The off-platform move | "Message me directly next time — it is cheaper for you" | Strips your billing record and every platform protection from check 1 |
| The question harvest | They have asked six questions and answered none | You are paying per minute to be interviewed |

The full taxonomy — twelve flags plus the cold-reading playbook underneath them — is in [how to spot a fake psychic](/guides/how-to-spot-fake-psychic/). Read it once before your first session; it is cheaper than learning it live.

### 13. Do you have an exit plan before you need one?

**Ending a bad session is a mechanical skill, and the time to learn it is before you are inside one.** The script is one sentence: *"I am going to end it here — thank you for your time."* Then disconnect. You owe no justification, and any reader who demands one has confirmed the decision.

Afterwards, two procedures. First, if the session was genuinely mis-sold — fear hooks, guarantees, bait behavior — use the platform's satisfaction program: all three majors compensate bad sessions with credit, subject to caps and windows that change, so check the current terms. Second, if the session felt fine but a prediction later fails, that is a different problem with its own protocol — [what to do when a psychic prediction does not come true](/guides/psychic-prediction-didnt-come-true/) — and the wrong reflex is immediate re-booking to chase the timeline. Rebooking discipline in general (one reader, one question, weeks apart) is covered in [how often you should get a reading](/guides/how-often-psychic-reading/).

## Which Checks Matter Most for You?

All thirteen apply to everyone, but the failure modes differ by situation. Where to concentrate:

| Your situation | Highest-priority checks | The reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| First reading ever | 1, 5, 6, 7, 13 | You have no calibration yet — buy cheaply, cap hard, and pre-plan the exit |
| Returning with one specific question | 3, 4, 6, 10, 11 | Precision is the whole game; drift and vagueness are your loss modes |
| A big decision (marriage, move, career change) | 3, 4, 6, 9, 13 | Treat the reading as one input among several — never the deciding one |
| On a tight budget | 2, 5, 7, 11, 12 | Newer mid-range readers plus the offers beat premium readers at list price |

## FAQs

### How much should a first psychic reading cost?

With a new-client offer, a legitimate 15–20 minute first session costs roughly $1 on Keen (5 minutes for $1), about $30–40 on Kasamba (3 free minutes plus 50% off, at a $4/min reader), or about $50 on Purple Garden (after the $30 credit), as of September 2026. Without an offer, expect $45–150 at typical rates of $3–8/min. More than $100 for a first session with an untested reader is overpaying for an uncalibrated bet — spend the savings on a second session if the first one earns it.

### Is chat, phone, or video better for a first reading?

Chat, for most first-timers, for three reasons: it leaves a written transcript you can re-read cold the next day; its slower pace protects a per-minute budget; and it strips out vocal cues, making the session a cleaner test of what the reader actually perceives. Phone adds speed and nuance once you trust a reader. Video adds presence — Purple Garden specializes in it — but it is the most expensive way to run an experiment.

### Are more expensive psychics more accurate?

No reliable correlation exists above the platform's baseline quality floor. Per-minute price tracks demand, seniority, and self-positioning more than measured accuracy. Across our 150+ published single-advisor reviews, several of the strongest performers sit in the $3–6/min mid-range, while some $20+/min readers persist on reputation alone. Price tells you how booked a reader is, not how often they are right.

### What if a psychic's prediction does not come true?

Do not immediately re-book to chase the timeline — that is the single most expensive reflex in this market. First separate the failure type: a wrong outcome, a slipped timeline, or a prediction distorted by your own actions after hearing it. Each has a different explanation and response, and the full protocol is laid out in [what to do when a prediction fails](/guides/psychic-prediction-didnt-come-true/).

### Can I get a refund for a bad psychic reading?

Usually as site credit, not cash. Kasamba, Keen, and Purple Garden all run satisfaction-style programs that compensate genuinely bad sessions with credit toward another reading, subject to caps and time windows that change periodically — verify the current terms before you book. This is the practical payoff of check 1: an established platform gives you a process; an independent operator gives you nothing.

### How do I know if a psychic is scamming me mid-session?

Apply the five flags from check 12: a fear hook ending in a paid "removal," a guaranteed outcome, urgency pressure to re-book, an attempt to move you off the platform, or a reader who asks more questions than they answer. Any one of them ends the session on its own. The fastest single test: if the session ends with you owing more than the per-minute rate times the minutes, something other than a reading was being sold. The complete twelve-flag breakdown is in [how to spot a fake psychic](/guides/how-to-spot-fake-psychic/).
